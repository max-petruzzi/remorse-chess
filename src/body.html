    How remorse works

    Normal chess rules, with one change: pieces feel guilt.

    1. Capturing makes a piece guilt-ridden. It gets a red token underneath.
    2. Guilt-ridden pieces are pacifists. They move as usual but cannot capture. Because they cannot capture, they also do not give check. A king may step right next to a guilty queen.
    3. Bishops absolve. Move a bishop onto any square touching a guilty piece of your side, including diagonals, and that piece is forgiven. That uses your turn. Every guilty friend touching the bishop is forgiven at once. A bishop already standing next to a guilty piece can use the Absolve in place button instead of moving.
    4. Bishops forgive themselves. A bishop that captures is guilty through your next turn, so it cannot capture or absolve on that turn. When that turn ends it is clean again. A dashed gold ring marks a bishop that is cleansing itself.
    5. No bishops, no forgiveness. Once your bishops are gone, guilt is permanent until a pawn promotes to a bishop.
    6. Checkmate wins. Stalemate, threefold repetition, the 50-move rule and insufficient material are draws.

    The original rules leave a few details open. This game uses these readings: a promotion that captures produces a guilty piece; a guilty king cannot capture either; capturing en passant counts as a capture; a guilt-ridden pawn stays guilt-ridden when it promotes (a guilty new bishop cleanses itself after one turn). If no pawns or bishops remain and every piece but the kings is guilt-ridden, the game is drawn, since nothing can ever capture again.
